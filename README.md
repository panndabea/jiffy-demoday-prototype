## Theory of Change for Jiffy

The **theory of change** framework from Abdullah&nbsp;Zeyn&nbsp;Hassan's workshop forces teams to map the causal chain from what they build to what changes in the world.  Inputs are what the team has, activities are what the team does, outputs are the direct results, outcomes are the changes for people, and impact is the broader systemic shift.  Applying this rigor to Jiffy reveals where the current pitch stops at features and how to move toward real change.

## Brutal critique — destroying the current pitch

Zeyn&nbsp;Hassan warns that founders conflate outputs with outcomes and pitch generic features instead of the change those features unlock.  Jiffy's current landing page falls into this trap.  Here's how a cynical Reddit or Hacker&nbsp;News thread would tear it apart:

1. **"So it's just Slido with a different coat of paint?"**  Every claim — QR codes, polls, Q&amp;A — already exists in Mentimeter, Slido or even Zoom.  Without showing why this solves a deeper problem, it feels like yet another widget.
2. **"No app download sounds nice until my phone's browser crashes."**  The site touts no app and instant joining:contentReference[oaicite:12]{index=12}, but it ignores real‑world friction: spotty conference Wi‑Fi, older browsers, accessibility needs.  People will drop off the moment it glitches.
3. **"Engagement isn't the problem, boring content is."**  If a presentation is dull, no amount of polls will save it.  Without helping hosts craft meaningful interactions, the tool risks being a gimmick.
4. **"GDPR headache? You're still collecting behaviour data."**  Claiming no email collection solves GDPR:contentReference[oaicite:13]{index=13} overlooks the fact that IP addresses, response data and potentially personal questions are still sensitive.
5. **"As a participant I don't want homework after your talk."**  Sending follow‑up links and files instantly:contentReference[oaicite:14]{index=14} might feel like spam.  Who asked for more emails from events?

Skeptical investors would raise tough questions:

1. **"What's the moat?"**  Polling and Q&amp;A features are commodity.  Without proprietary tech or network effects, bigger players can copy it overnight.
2. **"How do you monetise without turning into yet another SaaS subscription?"**  The pricing section ("Mini" vs. "Custom") is vague:contentReference[oaicite:15]{index=15}.  Investors will worry about low willingness to pay and high churn when free alternatives exist.
3. **"How will you survive when the hybrid‑event boom fades?"**  Engagement tools saw a spike during pandemic‑era remote events.  As in‑person events return, the novelty may wear off.

Reasons it might fail:

1. **Saturated market and switching costs.**  Event organisers already use established tools; getting them to switch requires a 10× improvement, not just matching features.
2. **Dependence on reliable connectivity.**  Poor Wi‑Fi or older devices can kill the experience.  The promise of "no app download" is moot when half the audience can't connect.
3. **Lack of behavioural change.**  Presenters may buy the tool but still fall back on old habits.  Without embedding new rituals, the product risks becoming shelfware.

## Real "wow" insight

Digging past the output ("we built a live facilitation system") into the outcome reveals the true job to be done: **people dread wasting time in one‑way meetings and feel unheard.**  The strongest transformation Jiffy can offer is not just "live polls," it's **the end of passive participation.**

Who actually benefits?  Introverts and junior employees who hesitate to speak up, remote attendees who feel disconnected, and presenters who fear losing the room.  When these groups use a frictionless interaction tool, they experience **belonging and agency** instead of boredom and anxiety.  The emotional job solved is **being seen and having one's time respected.**

Reframed, Jiffy isn't an "event platform."  It's **a cultural hack that turns every meeting into a two‑way conversation**.  It's how a shy intern asks the CEO a question without fearing retaliation.  It's how a conference attendee finds collaborators instead of doom‑scrolling.  It's how organisations respect the collective intelligence of their people.

1. **User / employee:**  *When a shy team member uses Jiffy during an all‑hands, they can ask questions and vote anonymously on their phone:contentReference[oaicite:17]{index=17}, which means they feel heard without the anxiety of speaking up and the meeting becomes more inclusive.*
2. **Investor:**  *When global enterprises roll Jiffy out across 1&nbsp;000 offices, meeting engagement rises and average meeting time drops by 20%, which means companies save millions in wasted hours and their workforce retains and acts on information.*
3. **Partner / event organiser:**  *When a conference organiser runs sessions with Jiffy, attendees stay engaged before, during and after:contentReference[oaicite:18]{index=18}, which means sponsors see higher ROI, sessions generate actionable feedback and repeat attendance increases.*

## Three reinvented product directions

1. **From tool to community catalyst.**  Instead of stopping at in‑session polls, Jiffy could automatically spin up a micro‑community for each event where attendees continue the conversation, exchange resources and form lasting connections.  This creates a network effect (participants invite others to stay connected) and builds habit beyond the event.  The outcome is that *events turn into communities*, not one‑off bursts of attention.  It's 10× better because it solves the loneliness of conferences and makes organisers indispensable.

2. **Adaptive meetings powered by AI.**  Integrate real‑time sentiment and attention analysis.  When engagement drops, Jiffy suggests switching to a quiz, breaking into small groups or shortening the slide deck.  Presenters no longer guess — they adapt on the fly.  This spreads because people brag about "the AI that rescued our meeting," creating a viral loop.  The outcome is meetings that respond to the room, not predetermined agendas.  It's 10× better because it turns wasted time into productive dialogue.

3. **Participation as currency.**  Introduce a gamified system where active participants earn micro‑credentials or tokens redeemable for learning credits, conference discounts or charitable donations.  Visibility of participation scores fosters healthy competition and social proof.  This spreads through leaderboards and shareable badges.  The outcome is *intrinsic motivation* to engage.  It's 10× better because it makes engagement rewarding rather than obligatory.

## Vision if Jiffy wins

Imagine a world five years from now where Jiffy's cultural hack took hold.  The phrase "death by PowerPoint" becomes archaic.  Every meeting begins with a QR code and ends with a snapshot of collective insights.  Introverts regularly influence decisions without stepping up to a microphone.  Conference organisers report higher satisfaction and lower attrition because attendees feel their voices mattered.  Companies eliminate unnecessary meetings and shorten the rest because engagement data shows when to stop.  The impact is not a clever app — it's the **normalisation of participatory culture**.  People stop wasting hours in passive lectures and instead co‑create experiences.  That's the change worth fighting for.
